swing-quant  ·  research note 2010–2026

The cash was doing the work

Sixteen years of daily bars, two markets, one validated swing-trading system, and four passive baselines. The system beat the index in Brazil and lost to it in the United States — and in Brazil most of what looked like trading skill turned out to be interest on money it never invested.

THE SETUP

Two contenders, neither of them a strawman

The question sounds simple. Is actively trading a quantitative swing system better than buying an index and leaving it alone? Almost every attempt to answer it goes wrong in the same place, and it is not the place people expect.

The trading side here is a real production system, not a curve-fitted demo. Two sleeves that each passed a ten-point validation protocol — walk-forward, out-of-sample, Monte Carlo, block bootstrap, cost stress at three times the modelled rate, and a random-entry baseline: a Donchian breakout on Brazilian large caps, and cross-sectional momentum on the S&P 500. Signals fire at the close of day D and fill at the open of D+1. Costs are charged on both legs. Position size comes from risk, not conviction.

The investing side is four baselines rather than one, because "the index" is a narrower question than it sounds: index buy-and-hold, an equal-weight portfolio of the same universe, the risk-free rate (CDI in Brazil, T-bills in the US), and — the one that turns out to matter — a passive portfolio deliberately held at the same average equity exposure as the swing book.

On the risk-free rate. Sharpe and Sortino below are computed against the actual daily rate series, not against zero. Over this period CDI compounded at 9.8% a year. A zero-rate Sharpe would flatter every Brazilian strategy by roughly half a point and would rank a 5% strategy above cash that paid 10%.

Read the two panels as separate experiments, because they disagree. The Ibovespa returned 5.7% a year while CDI paid 9.8% — sixteen years of equity risk, a 48.6% drawdown and 6.7 years underwater, to finish behind a money-market account. Against that backdrop nearly anything looks good. In the United States the opposite: SPY compounded at 14.2% while T-bills paid 1.3%, and the swing book's 10.5% lost by four points a year.

And yet the swing book has a higher Sharpe ratio than the index in both markets — 0.39 against −0.05 in Brazil, 1.10 against 0.79 in the US. Better risk-adjusted return, worse terminal wealth, in the same table. That contradiction is the study.

FINDING 01

The comparison everyone reaches for is broken

A swing book sized by risk does not hold stocks. It holds a little bit of stocks and a very large pile of cash.

Position size comes out of a risk budget: half a percent of equity divided by two ATRs of stop distance, then capped by a maximum weight per name, by participation in traded volume, and by available cash. Run that arithmetic across a whole portfolio and the book is roughly 72% cash on average, in both markets.

Comparing a 28%-invested portfolio to a 100%-invested index is not a test of trading skill. It is a test of how equities happened to do.

If stocks rose, the fully invested portfolio wins by construction. If they fell, the 28%-invested one wins by construction. Neither outcome says anything about whether the entry and exit rules added value. The fix is to hold the exposure constant: put w = 28% in the index and the rest at the risk-free rate, rebalanced daily. Same average equity exposure, no timing, no skill — just a passive portfolio that happens to be mostly cash, exactly like the swing book.

FINDING 02

In Brazil, 56% of the return was interest

The backtest engine pays 0% on idle cash. A real broker account does not.

Leaving idle cash at zero is the right call inside a validation protocol — it is conservative, and it stops a strategy from being approved on the strength of the money it never deployed. It is the wrong call when the question is "trading versus investing," because a Brazilian broker paying 100% of CDI turns 72% of the book into a money-market fund. So the honest curve credits it: each day's portfolio return, plus cash weight × risk-free.

Once the cash earns its keep, it becomes fair — and uncomfortable — to ask how much of the result came from the trades and how much came from the money sitting behind them.

Of the Brazilian strategy's 12.35% a year, 6.92 points came from interest and 5.08 from the trades. Switch the cash yield off and the same system makes 5.1% — losing to a savings account by 4.7 points a year.

The same decomposition in the United States gives 9%, because that sleeve ran through a decade of zero rates. The strategy is identical in spirit; the environment is the whole difference. Which reframes what the product actually is:

A swing book run at half a percent of risk per trade is not an equity strategy. It is a money-market fund with a small equity overlay — and in a high-rate country, most of its return is the fund, not the overlay. It should be benchmarked, marketed and taxed as one.

FINDING 03

Most of the "edge over the index" is a rate signal

And the part that survives holding cash constant is small, steady, and real.

If a large share of the swing return is interest, then how good swing trading looks should rise and fall with the interest rate — and it should stop doing so once the comparison holds the same cash. Sorting every overlapping one-year window into quartiles by the prevailing rate tests exactly that.

Brazil gives a clean result. Measured against the index, the edge lurches from −0.3% to +10.7% depending on the quartile. Measured against a blend holding the same cash — and therefore earning the same interest — it sits in a narrow band of +2.3% to +4.5%, with a correlation to the rate level of −0.03.

The real edge is 3.2 percentage points a year, not the 6.7 the index comparison advertises. Roughly half the apparent skill was the Selic rate.

The US table does not decompose as cleanly: its blend-relative edge still climbs with rates, correlation +0.48. The honest reading is confounding rather than causation — the high-rate quartile in the US is 2023–2026, which is also when this particular momentum strategy did its best work. With one path through history there is no way to separate the two, and I would not claim the rate caused it.

FINDING 04

The risk numbers run the other way

Everything so far has been about return. Drawdown is what decides whether a person stays in the seat.

In Brazil the index lost money over 39% of all one-year windows and its worst year was −36%. The swing book lost money in 3% of windows, and its worst year was −4.1%. In the US the index's worst year was −19.7% against the swing book's −9.5%, and maximum drawdown was 33.7% against 13.0%.

The conditional rows carry the mechanism. When the index was down over a year, the Brazilian swing book still averaged +8.0% against the index's −9.8%. When the index was up, it made 15.9% against 18.6%. It gives up some of the upside and nearly all of the downside — which is exactly what holding 72% cash does, plus whatever a trend-following exit rule adds on top.

So the swing system is not worse. It is a different asset: lower risk, lower return. Whether that trade is worth making has a clean test.

FINDING 05

At equal return, whose risk is worse?

A better Sharpe ratio is only useful if you can scale it up.

Lever the swing book until its return matches the index, then compare the risk. This scales the strategy's excess return over the risk-free rate, which scales volatility and drawdown roughly in proportion — arithmetic that ignores borrowing spread, margin calls and path risk, all of which make the real levered version worse than this.

In Brazil the question does not arise — the swing book beat the index outright at a quarter of the volatility. In the US it does, and the answer is genuinely interesting: to match SPY's 14.2%, the sleeve needs about 1.40× leverage, and levered to that point it would have run at 11.5% volatility with an 18.1% maximum drawdown, against the index's 17.1% and 33.7%. Same return, roughly half the drawdown.

That is a real advantage. It also requires running 1.4× leverage on a book that trades 228 times a year, financed at a broker's margin rate, through a 2020-style gap. The arithmetic works; the arithmetic is not the part that fails.

FINDING 06

Costs bruise. Taxes are structural.

One of these is a modelling assumption. The other is a permanent asymmetry between trading and holding.

Backtested costs are a guess, so the sensible thing is to bracket them. Multiplying the whole cost model by 0×, 1×, 2× and 3× takes Brazil from 12.35% to 10.84% and the US from 10.52% to 8.40% — about half a point and seven-tenths of a point per turn of the dial. The US sleeve is more fragile because it trades 228 times a year against Brazil's 37.

Taxes are the part almost every backtest omits, and unlike costs they are not a calibration question. Trading realises gains and pays tax on them every month; buying and holding defers indefinitely and pays once, at the end, out of a base that compounded untaxed the whole way.

A swing book generates two taxable streams, under two regimes. Trading gains: 15% on each month's net gain in Brazil, losses carried forward, with the R$20,000 monthly-sales exemption applied where it genuinely applies; a flat 25% stands in for US short-term gains taxed as ordinary income. And interest on the cash sleeve, taxed on the Brazilian regressiva table, which bottoms out at 15% past two years — either on redemption, or twice a year by come-cotas if the cash sits in a fund.

The drag runs about 0.8 points a year in Brazil and 2.4 in the US for the swing book, against 0.6 and 1.0 for buying and holding. Turnover is taxed; patience is deferred. Brazil's number is smaller than it looks because losses carry forward and absorb later gains — this system wins only 38% of its trades — and because the R$20,000 exemption genuinely bites in the early years, while the book is still small.

Simplified, and not tax advice: no wrappers, no day-trade regime, no 0.005% withholding, and a flat stand-in rate for US short-term gains.
FINDING 07

Nobody actually invests a lump sum

And when you model what people really do, one number moves by 3.6 points.

Every figure so far assumes a single deposit on day one and no contact with the account for sixteen and a half years. The realistic pattern is a fixed monthly contribution, which is money-weighted rather than time-weighted: the returns that matter are the ones that happened while there was money in the account.

The ranking survives. But dollar-cost averaging into the Ibovespa returns an IRR of 9.27% against a lump-sum CAGR of 5.7% — because Brazil's bad years came early, when a monthly contributor had almost nothing invested, and the good years came late, when the account was full. Timing luck, not skill, worth 3.6 points a year.

Which is a useful warning about the entire exercise: a single sixteen-year path produces point estimates that are far less precise than they look printed to two decimals.

THE OTHER COLUMN

What it costs to run

The Brazilian sleeve asks for 37 trades a year — 74 orders — plus a screening run every session. The US sleeve asks for 228 trades, 456 orders. The passive alternative asks for one order, ever.

At the Brazilian sleeve's residual edge over a cash-matched passive portfolio, 3.2 points a year, a R$100,000 account earns roughly R$3,200 a year for that work. Whether that is a good hourly rate depends entirely on the size of the account — which is the real argument for automating the whole thing, and the real limit on swing trading as a strategy for small capital.

BOUNDS

What would change these numbers

Survivorship. The universe is today's index membership walked backwards. Dropped companies are missing, and they were dropped for doing badly. This helps the passive baselines most, and the equal-weight baseline enormously — treat its 17.7% US figure as an unreachable ceiling, not a portfolio.
One path. Every point estimate is a single draw from 16.6 years. The rolling-window sections give some spread; they are not a distribution.
In-sample parameters. Donchian's 40/10 lookbacks and momentum's 189/21 were selected by the validation protocol on the training portion — but selected on this data. The passive baselines have no parameters to select.
Cash yield is a broker decision. The Brazilian result assumes the broker pays 100% of CDI on idle balances. Many do; some pay nothing. At 0% the same system makes 5.1% a year instead of 12.4%.
Rates are not stable. The Brazilian result rests on a period when CDI averaged 9.8%. At a Selic near 2%, as in 2020, more than half the swing return simply disappears.
No borrowing costs. The leverage section is arithmetic, not a financing model.
CONCLUSION

Four things the data says

01

The naive question is unanswerable

"Did the strategy beat the index?" cannot be answered, because the two do not hold the same equity risk. A book sized by volatility is 72% cash; comparing it to a fully invested index measures the market's mood over the sample, not the rules.

02

The real edge is half the advertised one

Against a passive portfolio holding the same average exposure, the Brazilian sleeve earned 3.2 percentage points a year — stably, across every interest-rate regime. Real, and roughly half of what the index comparison claims.

03

Risk-adjusted, the trader wins; in wealth, the investor usually does

Higher Sharpe in both markets and a third of the drawdown — and still US$106k against the index's US$182k, because the risk it declined to take was risk that paid. Converting Sharpe into money needs leverage, and leverage is where paper strategies go to die.

04

The environment decides, not the discipline

Same engine, same risk rules, same discipline — a clear win in Brazil and a clear loss in the US. The difference is mostly CDI at 9.8% against T-bills at 1.3%. Anyone reporting a Brazilian swing-trading record from the last decade is reporting, in large part, the Selic rate.

Not "trading beats investing," nor the reverse. Trading buys a different risk profile at a real but modest price — and in Brazil, the money-market leg has been doing most of the work.

Method. All figures computed from 2,309,558 daily bars in the project's DuckDB store, using its production backtest engine — same costs, same sizing, same risk rules that run live.
